Common pattern (all products): a strong weekly cycle. Sales concentrate on Tuesday and Wednesday and are almost zero on Friday (Product B never sells on Friday). The lag-5 autocorrelation (0.41 to 0.66) is much larger than the lag-1 one (-0.16 to 0.16), so "same weekday last week" carries more information than "yesterday". Daily data are also very noisy (CV 0.9 to 2.3) with occasional large spikes (the top 5% of days make 17% to 43% of the volume). There is no visible month-of-year seasonality in the two years available, so I could not test annual seasonality.

  • Product A - high volume, almost no zero days, clear downward trend: 796k units in 2011, 626k in 2012, and only 87k in the first 3 months of 2013 (about 29k/month versus 52k/month in 2012). The level is falling, so a forecast must adapt and not rely on the full history.
  • Product B - low, stable level (about 5,700 units/month, no trend), 26% of days with zero sales (all Fridays plus some other days). Forecast should be based on weekday behaviour; the level can be averaged over a long window.
  • Product C - intermittent demand (75% zero days, ADI = 4.0), concentrated on Wednesday (index 3.6), with an upward trend (1,090 units in 2011, 2,509 in 2012 and 924 in Q1-2013 alone). Percent error measures are not meaningful here.
  • Product D - high volume and the most volatile (CV 1.36, huge spikes up to 13,200). Growth in 2011-2012 (362k to 475k units), with a peak around Jan-May 2012, then a fall since Sep-2012 (about 24k/month in the last 3 full months versus about 40k in 2012).

Meaning for the forecast: a single flat level (cumulative mean, long moving average) is wrong for A, C and D because the level moves; a naive forecast is wrong everywhere because yesterday is a weak predictor of today. The forecast must (1) be at weekday level and (2) follow the level slowly. For C (intermittent) I also tested Croston.

2. Methods tested and why

For each product I ran a rolling one-step-ahead back-test over the last 120 business days (each forecast uses only earlier data). Methods: Naive, Cumulative mean, Moving average (N = 5, N = 20), simple Exponential Smoothing (alpha = 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3), Seasonal naive (same weekday last week), Weekday mean (last 4, 8, 12 same weekdays), Weekday-index x Exponential Smoothing (alpha = 0.05, 0.1, 0.2; ES on the deseasonalised series, then re-multiplied by the weekday index), and Croston (intermittent demand, alpha = 0.1). The first four simple methods are those studied in the course; the weekday versions are the same ideas applied to what the pattern analysis showed.

# Python (pandas / numpy) - R Markdown style chunk, rendered with output
# one-step-ahead evaluation on the last 120 business days (H = 120)
def wd_es(h, dow_hist, dow_next, a):
    idx = [h[dow_hist == j].mean() for j in range(5)]      # weekday index (mean of weekday / grand mean)
    idx = np.maximum(idx / np.mean(idx), 1e-6)
    ds = h / idx[dow_hist]                                 # deseasonalised history
    f = ds[0]
    for v in ds[1:]:
        f = f + a * (v - f)                                # F(t+1) = F(t) + a (D(t) - F(t))
    return f * idx[dow_next]                               # re-seasonalise for the target weekday

Accuracy measures: mean error ME = mean(D - F) (negative = over-forecast), mean absolute error MAE, mean squared error MSE, and WAPE = sum|e| / sum(D). I use WAPE instead of MAPE as the main percent measure because MAPE divides by actual demand and explodes on near-zero days (Product B and C have many zero days; the MAPE on non-zero days is shown in the full tables only).

Why these choices. A and D: ES on the deseasonalised series with a small alpha (0.1 for A, whose level is moving fast down; 0.05 for D, which is the noisiest). B: alpha = 0.05 because the level is stable and the noise is large, so the forecast should average a lot. C: a plain mean of the same weekday in the last 12 weeks beat ES and Croston; Croston was designed for intermittent demand that has no weekday pattern, but here the zeros follow the weekday (Wednesday), so the weekday mean is better.

3. Conclusions

Do the methods work? The weekday-based methods work clearly better than the simple ones from the course: MSE is lower by 64% (A), 65% (B), 83% (C) and 75% (D) compared with the naive forecast, and by 39%, 28%, 59% and 51% compared with the best simple course method per product (MA(20) for A, C and D; cumulative mean for B). At weekly level (sum of the daily forecasts) the WAPE is 33% (A), 33% (B), 16% (C) and 24% (D), against 34%, 37%, 20% and 38% for a naive weekly forecast. At daily level the WAPE is still 49% to 65%, so these products cannot be forecast precisely day by day: spikes (orders of 10,000+ units) are not predictable from the history alone.

Weaknesses and bias. Product A has a negative ME (-223): the forecast is too high because the level keeps falling and ES lags a declining series. Product B has a positive ME (+41), C a small one (+0.9) and D (+44) a small under-forecast. No method can see a promotion or a customer order coming.

How to improve: (1) add a trend component (Holt, or damped trend) for A, C and D; (2) use causal data such as promotions, tenders, customer orders and stock-outs to explain the spikes; (3) forecast at weekly level and split to days with the weekday index when the use is replenishment; (4) separate large one-off orders from base demand and forecast them apart; (5) re-optimise alpha on a regular schedule (rolling), track a tracking signal and re-fit when it drifts; (6) obtain more than 2 years of data to test annual seasonality; (7) for C, aggregate to week and use prediction intervals.

4. Next steps: from forecast to Supply Chain Plan

  1. Convert the forecasts into a demand plan. Roll the daily forecasts up to weeks and months (aggregation reduces error), add the safety margin from the back-test errors, and review them with Sales and Marketing (S&OP demand review) to include promotions, launches and the decline of A.
  2. Set inventory targets. Use forecast error (MSE, WAPE) to compute safety stock by service level (high for A and D volumes; for C, hold a small stock covering the Wednesday demand) and reorder point / EOQ for each product.
  3. Plan supply and manufacturing. Compare demand with capacity and lead time (production, packaging, quality release for pharmaceuticals), build the master production schedule and the material requirements for active ingredient and packaging, and check shelf-life limits.
  4. Plan distribution. Match the strong Tuesday-Wednesday peak with warehouse labour and transport capacity, and set delivery schedules.
  5. Run the S&OP cycle and monitor. Meet monthly, compare plan with actual, follow ME/MAE/WAPE and tracking signal per product, and re-forecast when accuracy drops or the level changes (especially A and D, which are now falling).
